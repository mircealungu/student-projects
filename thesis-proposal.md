# The Project Proposal: the abstract you can't write yet

A proposal has the same skeleton as  [the famous four-sentence abstract](thesis-abstract.md) by Kent Beck. The
difference is that the third sentence — the startling one — is still a question, and you have
to say how you plan to answer it.

So: the same four elements, with two insertions.

1. **The problem.**
2. **Why the problem is a problem.** Convince the reader. Why should they bother reading this thesis. Why will you bother working on it? Why is it important?
3. **The research question.** (In the abstract this will become a claim. For now it's Kent
   Beck's third sentence, reversed — see the note at the bottom of `thesis-abstract.md`.)
4. **What you expect the answer to be.** You are allowed to turn out to be wrong. 
But at least you should have an expectation.
5. **How you will find out.** The method, the data you'll collect, and — crucially — what
   result would tell you your hypothesis was wrong.
6. **The implication, if it holds.**

This is not a section template. You don't have to hand in six labelled headings. But try to start with six sentences first.


## A worked example

Take the paper Beck's advice comes from and put it back into proposal form, before the work
was done:

> Close to nine out of ten papers submitted to OOPSLA are rejected. Reviewers report that
> most of those rejections are not caused by weak ideas but by papers whose argument is hard
> to locate, so good work is lost and the field advances more slowly than it could. **Can a
> fixed four-step structure for the abstract increase a paper's chance of acceptance?** I
> expect that it can, and substantially, because programme committee members triage on the
> abstract alone before reading anything else. To test this, I will take rejected submissions
> from previous years, rewrite their abstracts in the four-step form while leaving the body
> untouched, and ask experienced reviewers to sort original and rewritten versions into
> accept/reject piles without knowing which is which. If the rewritten abstracts are sorted
> no differently from the originals, the conjecture fails. If they are sorted more favourably,
> then a mechanical, teachable edit — one that costs an author an hour — is worth more to
> acceptance than most of what we currently tell students about writing.

One paragraph. Every element present.

Let us untangle it: 

1. **The problem** — nine out of ten submissions are rejected.
2. **Why it's a problem** — the rejections are caused by structure rather than by weak ideas,
   so good work is lost.
3. **The question** — can a fixed four-step abstract raise the acceptance rate?
4. **The expectation** — yes, substantially, because committees triage on the abstract.
5. **The method, and what would refute it** — rewrite the abstracts of previously rejected
   papers, have reviewers sort them blind; identical sorting kills the conjecture.
6. **The implication** — an hour of mechanical editing would be worth more than most of the
   writing advice we currently give.


Notice that element 3 is a question here and will become a statement in the final abstract —
that is the whole relationship between the two documents. Notice also that element 5 already
runs to two sentences: the method needs room even in the compressed version.


## Then expand

The six sentences are the skeleton, not the submission. Once they hold, each one becomes the
first sentence of a paragraph and the rest of that paragraph is support: the evidence behind
the problem, the numbers behind the cost, the design of the study, the threats to its
validity. 

The paragraphs will not come out equal. Element 5 is usually the longest, because method needs more room; element 6 is often still a single sentence. Expand each as far as it needs and no further. And don't be afraid to have a single-sentence paragraph. Sometimes, they are the most powerful. 

What you should not do is turn the six into six labelled headings. 

## Before you write: can you measure it?

Most weak proposals fail here rather than in the prose. If your question involves users
"understanding" something, or a tool being "usable", or a process being "improved", stop and
answer this first: **how would I detect it?** A task-based measure, a validated
questionnaire, a number in the logs, a coded interview transcript. If you cannot name the
instrument, you do not yet have element 5, and no amount of rewriting will produce it.

## The title

Give the document your working thesis title, not a label like *Preliminary Problem
Statement*. A title is the shortest form of your claim, so writing one is the same test as
the six sentences, only faster. And put your name under it. It's your's now :)

It is ok if it is provisional. You will rewrite it when you know how the work turned out. 
But make it say something. "Onboarding in Complex Language-Learning Applications" names a subject.
"Does Sequencing Onboarding Around the Learning Loop Improve Feature Adoption?" names the thing you're actually going to find out. Again, give your best effort estimation at the beginning. 
You'll iterate over it later. 


## This document is alive

Don't file the proposal away after it's approved. When the work is done, you will rewrite
sentence 3 from a question into a claim, drop element 5 into the methodology chapter, and
what remains is your abstract. If you find at that point that you cannot make the edit, the
proposal was describing a project other than the one you did — which is worth knowing before
you start writing the thesis, not after.
